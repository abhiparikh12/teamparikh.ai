Provisions Worth Knowing

▶StandardA new owner takes the lot as-is, including any prior architectural issues (§9.10)
This is common CC&R language: by accepting the deed, an owner accepts the lot's condition, including any architectural violations a prior owner left unresolved, whether or not they were disclosed. Nothing in this package points to one here. The sellers state that no improvements were made without required HOA approval (SPQ 14.F(2)). The board minutes show no violations or architectural concerns involving this lot; the committee reported "no concerns" in January 2025 and "no open applications" in October 2025.
This is routine. The HOA's resale disclosure package normally includes any notice of violation on the lot (Civil Code §4528), which will confirm it.
▶ProtectionLarge dues increases and special assessments require an owner vote (§8.7.6, §8.9.2)
Following Civil Code §5605, the board cannot raise regular dues by more than 20% in a year, or levy special assessments totaling more than 5% of the year's budgeted expenses, without a majority vote of the owners with a quorum of more than 50%. Emergencies under Civil Code §5610 are the exception. On a budget of roughly $340,000, the 5% limit is about $17,000 in total, or roughly $325 per home. Anything larger, such as a future paving or retaining-wall assessment, would go to an owner vote in which you would participate. Owners must be notified 30–60 days before any increase or special assessment is due.
▶ApprovalArchitectural approval is required for nearly everything outside — and you may owe a street-damage deposit
No building, fence, hedge, wall, patio cover, awning, outdoor lighting, antenna, landscaping or exterior change may begin, and nothing may be painted, without written approval from the Architectural Committee (§7.1). If you plan construction, the HOA may require a deposit against damage to the private streets, set after an engineer's pre-construction assessment. You are liable for any damage beyond the deposit (§7.13). Rooftop solar and EV chargers are protected by state law and cannot be prohibited, but they are subject to reasonable rules (§7.4.3–7.4.4).
▶LimitsBuilding envelope: two stories / 35 feet; 3,500 sq ft minimum home size (§5.7)
Homes are limited to two stories and 35 feet above average finished grade, and major roof lines may not exceed 30 feet. The primary residence must have at least 3,500 square feet of usable space, excluding garages. Lot 19 is not one of the exempt lots (28, 31, 41, 42 and 43). These limits matter if you plan an addition or ever rebuild.
▶RulesVehicles, pets, rentals and use
  • Vehicles (§5.19): The garage is the primary parking place and must hold the number of cars it was designed for. Boats, trailers, campers, RVs and anything too large for the garage may stay only 4 hours in any 48 for loading. No car may sit on the street more than 72 hours. Commercial vehicles cannot stay overnight unless kept in the garage or driveway. No car washing on common area. Violators can be towed.
  • Pets (§5.15): Up to three dogs, cats or birds combined. Pets must be leashed on common areas, and outdoor feeding of wildlife is not allowed.
  • Rentals (§6.1): Allowed only for the whole home, with a written lease of at least 30 days and notice to the board. Short-term rentals are effectively prohibited.
  • Use (§5.6, §5.8): Residential only, with limited home businesses allowed. Qualified family day care and residential care homes are permitted by law.
  • Signs (§5.17): One for-sale or for-rent sign is allowed, and noncommercial signs up to 9 sq ft. Political signs may go up no more than 30 days before an election.
  • Noise and neighbors (§5.12): The HOA is not obligated to step into disputes that are solely between neighbors. Complaints must be made in writing after informal efforts to resolve them.
▶EasementThe private road carries a public pedestrian and bicycle trail easement (§4.4.8)
Contra Costa County, the Town of Danville and members of the public have a non-exclusive easement to walk and bike over Parcel D, the private street. The HOA maintains it, not the county or town. An adjoining owner also has recorded road-access rights and pays the association $6,200 a year. A gated community here does not mean the road is closed to the public on foot or bicycle.
